# Sheet-Metal-NC-Punch-Clamp-Avoidance-Safety-Architecture (v1.2)
— Clamp and Die Damage Prevention for Sheet Metal NC Punching/Shearing Machines, Fail-Safe for Sensor Cable Internal Micro-Breakage and Zero-Point Alignment Shift via Clamp Warping/Distortion, Dual-Track Failure Data Code Management, and Local AI Edge Autonomous Survival Control Architecture

## 1. Document Purpose and Scope
The purpose of this document is to mitigate physical interference, grazing, and collisions between tooling faces and clamps during X/Y table positioning on NC sheet metal punching and shearing complex machinery (including manufacturer designations such as ADTECH NCT series, Amada Vipros, Trumpf TruPunch, regardless of sheet material, punching/shearing process, or controller operation modes such as Manual/MDI/JOG/AUTO). Such incidents cause clamp/die damage, turret disk jamming due to chipping debris, zero-point (datum) alignment shift due to accumulated shock fatigue, and unannounced operational failures caused by visually unidentifiable sensor cable internal micro-breakages. Without requiring complete equipment replacement, this architecture respects existing prior art patents and field craftsmanship accumulated by manufacturers and operators. Extending beyond simple software keepout zones or empirical zone calculation prior art (such as US 5400259), this architecture combines commanded vs. measured position cross-validation, micro-current impedance sensing fail-safe mechanisms, and eFPGA hardware interlocks to assist continuous functional safety.

Key functionalities include dual cross-validation between programmed clamp relative position (commanded) and replaceable clamp-mounted material detection sensors (measured), zero-point shift detection and recalibration interlocks caused by clamp warping, a 200mm fixed safety line non-punching bypass motion logic (independent of specific G-code numbers), ultra-low latency detection of internal cable micro-breakages and intermittent signal dropouts, dual-track failure data code preservation logging, work position reentry control, eFPGA hardwired STO (Safe Torque Off) and SS1 (Safe Stop 1) physical interlock integration, M80/M81 clamp lift-up linkage, smart terminal mesh interoperation, Quiet Assist during cumulative operator fatigue, and T-Reg progressive deceleration. Grounded in the soma-moa philosophy (Safety-II and Just Culture), the system initiates physical interlock interventions only upon detecting hazard signatures to safely halt machine movement.

## 3. Failure Mode Analysis Based on Field Observations
* OSHA & ANSI B11 Classification — NC sheet metal punching/shearing machines fall under OSHA 1910.212 (General requirements for all machines), ANSI B11.0 (Safety of Machinery - General Requirements), and ANSI B11.19 (Performance Requirements for Risk Reduction Measures). Physical interference between clamps moving at high speeds on X/Y tables and vertically driven turret tooling poses severe risks of equipment destruction and projectile debris injuries. (Note: Slide-driven power press standards ANSI B11.1 and OSHA 1910.217 govern reciprocating dies fixed to slide mechanisms and are distinct from rotating turret and X/Y table kinematics.)
* Failure Mode 1 — Manual NC Code Bypass Insertion Errors, Manual (MDI/JOG) Operation, and Human Error — In field operations, automated CAM programming coexists with manual MDI command entries, JOG handwheel positioning, and manual text-editor G-code bypass modifications. Single operator errors or visual misjudgments during manual positioning can lead directly to tool face grazing or heavy impacts against clamps.
* Failure Mode 2 — Zero-Point (Datum) Shift from Cumulative Fatigue and Clamp Warping — Even without outright clamp destruction, continuous punching shockwaves and micro-grazing cause cumulative structural fatigue, leading to clamp mount and jaw warping/distortion. This shifts the physical zero-point (datum), introducing dimensional inaccuracies across all punched holes and causing positioning paths to drift outside computed safety zones into secondary collisions.
* Failure Mode 3 — Non-Stall Micro-Grazing, Chipping Debris, and Turret Disk Jamming — Light scraping between tooling and clamp edges often fails to exceed servo motor over-torque thresholds, allowing machining to continue while bending clamp sensor brackets or dislodging metal chips into lower turret disk clearances, resulting in T-axis rotation jams and sheet ejection hazards.
* Failure Mode 4 — Internal Micro-Breakage of Sensor Cables and Intermittent Ghost Signals — Sensor cables mounted on clamps undergo continuous flexing on high-speed X/Y tables. While outer protective jacketing appears intact under visual inspection, internal copper strands frequently suffer micro-fractures. This induces intermittent open circuits or sub-millisecond signal dropouts during specific acceleration vectors, causing controllers to misinterpret material clamping status and trigger sudden collisions.
* Failure Mode 5 — Line Stops from Rail Detachment and Clamp Disassembly — Resolving clamp collisions requires removing clamp assemblies from linear guide rails, replacing damaged jaws, bolts, pins, and hydraulic fittings, and re-aligning mechanical parallelism upon remounting. Unplanned downtime ranges from 1 to 2 hours for basic component swaps up to 3 to 4 hours (240-minute upper cap) when diagnosing unconfirmed electrical/sensor faults such as internal cable micro-breakages.
* Failure Mode 5-1 — Two-Person Requirement for Hydraulic Release Verification — Because hydraulic clamps do not automatically disengage from rails due to friction and self-weight when depressurized, and because release foot pedals are located far from clamp mounting points on wide tables, clamp servicing routinely requires two operators (one operating pedals, one verifying mechanical release).
* Failure Mode 5-2 — Double Maintenance Labor from Temporary Component Reassembly — In the absence of immediate spare parts, field technicians often perform temporary repairs with partially damaged components to resume emergency production, followed by a second complete tear-down and re-alignment once genuine replacement parts arrive, doubling total labor hours.
* Failure Mode 5-3 — Exponential Line Stops from External After-Sales Service (AS) Dispatch — Complex damage requiring official manufacturer field service dispatches incurs technician travel time, schedule queue delays, and specialized parts procurement, extending downtime from half a day (4 to 6 hours) to multiple days.
* Failure Mode 6 — Cumulative Operator Fatigue during Continuous Shifts — Prolonged repetitive operations and night shifts induce operator cognitive fatigue, increasing the incidence of skipped NC code inspections or incorrect clamp position setups.
* Mitigation of Operational Side-Effects & Modern Governance — Operating under Safety-II and Just Culture principles, this architecture functions as a non-intrusive assistant (Quiet Assist), engaging eFPGA hardware interlocks to halt machine motion only when physical collision parameters are breached.

## 4. Field Organizational, Human Factors, and Equipment Aging Background (Informational)
> Note: The following contextual background explains the field environment necessitating automated safety systems and does not constitute technical claims of the invention.

* Informal Used Equipment Markets and Engineering Retrofits — The used machinery market relies heavily on informal technician networks. Small to mid-sized fabricators frequently choose to extend the operational life of existing or refurbished machinery through precision mechanical recalibration and electronic retrofits rather than committing capital to new equipment. Non-intrusive edge safety retrofits provide an economically viable means of elevating safety to modern standards on legacy machinery.
* Field Observation on Legacy Machine Manual Complementation and AS Linkage — Observations from the field author's experience operating ~10-year-old machinery indicate that operators routinely conducted manual spatial reviews before executing programs, adding manual bypass movements around clamp coordinates to prevent tool damage. Although CAM software contained clamp avoidance routines, incomplete coverage required manual operator complementation. Such manual inspection relies heavily on operator vigilance, creating risks of human error—a vulnerability explicitly noted in machine tool patent background literature (e.g., Yamazaki Mazak US 9229443 B2 regarding manual program editing risks). Collisions resulting from manual oversight directly trigger the AS dispatch dependencies described above. The 200mm fixed safety line logic aims to systematically mitigate these manual review dependencies. (Note: This narrative reflects historical field observations and does not evaluate specific current commercial software versions or generalize across all equipment.)
* AS Decision Workflows, Apprenticeships, and Operator Dual-Roles — In small fabricators, operators frequently handle setup, machining, and minor maintenance. When clamp damage occurs, experienced operators perform initial assessments; if internal repair is infeasible, management requests manufacturer AS dispatch. Junior operators assist visiting AS engineers during repairs, learning mechanical alignment and diagnostic skills through informal apprenticeship.
* AS Engineer Interaction and Administrative Procedures — AS engineers facilitate on-site technical knowledge transfer because mechanical clamp repairs are straightforward compared to complex servo or software troubleshooting. Repeated service visits build professional trust between plant staff and service providers. Formally, AS engineers brief plant management on failure causes and repair scopes, establishing administrative documentation for service billing.
* Field Observations on AS Purchase Bundles — Field accounts indicate that 10- to 20-year-old used machine acquisitions often include informal ongoing service support from vendors (AS bundling). Without such support, legacy equipment sustainability drops significantly. Service conditions vary widely across machine types, vendor channels, and regions.
* Equipment Aging, Used Machinery Exports, and Geographic Scalability — The probability of Failure Modes 1 through 6 increases with machine operational age. Accumulated fatigue increases clamp distortion (Failure Mode 2) and cable micro-breakages (Failure Mode 4). Development economics research (Barba Navaretti, Soloaga & Takacs, 1998/2000) documents how industrial capital goods extend their life cycles through export to developing markets matched with local operator skills, suggesting that the physical application scope of the retrofit safety architecture proposed in this whitepaper is not limited to domestic markets but can extend across the global machine tool ecosystem.

## 5. 200mm Fixed Safety Line, Dual-Sensor Fail-Safe, eFPGA/SS1 Interlock, and Time Loss Calculation
* 200mm Fixed Safety Line Avoidance Logic — Based on standard large turret tooling station dimensions (up to 114.3mm outer diameter), a minimum rotational clearance of 150mm is required to account for rotational inertia, machine vibration, and thermal expansion. Establishing a 200mm fixed safety line provides a universal clearance margin across diverse station layouts. Applying a conditional rule—`if measured clamp Y < 200 then issue bypass movement`—aims, under general conditions, to mitigate tooling interference across all stations in the turret.
* Commanded vs. Measured Dual Cross-Validation and Zero-Point Recalibration — The system continuously compares programmed clamp coordinates (commanded) against external material detection sensors (measured). Respecting prior art in empirical zone calculation (US 5400259), this architecture adds dual-channel cross-validation and electrical continuity gating before permitting motion execution (e.g., G01).
  * Mode Independence and Zero-Point Alignment Defense — Operates continuously across Manual (JOG/MDI) and Automatic (AUTO) modes. If accumulated punch fatigue alters the physical zero-point (datum) beyond allowable thresholds, the system halts execution and prompts zero-point alignment recalibration.
  * Internal Cable Micro-Breakage Fail-Safe — To catch unidentifiable internal strand breakages and transient open circuits during rapid acceleration, an eFPGA module monitors dynamic electrical continuity and micro-impedance variance. Detecting sub-1ms signal dropouts or impedance anomalies immediately triggers an NC Fail-Safe halt.
* eFPGA Hardwired STO and SS1 (Safe Stop 1) Inertial Deceleration Interlock — Upon detecting sensor mismatches, zero-point shifts, or internal cable breakages, issuing an immediate STO (Safe Torque Off, Stop Category 0) alone can cause high-mass X/Y tables to slide into collisions due to mechanical inertia. This architecture integrates SS1 (Safe Stop 1, Stop Category 1) control to execute controlled decelerated stopping before issuing STO, implemented directly via eFPGA hardware to meet functional safety requirements (IEC 61800-5-2, ISO 13849-1 PL d/e, IEC 61508/62061 SIL 2/3).
* Time Loss and Defensive Efficiency Framework — Enforcing a 200mm safety bypass adds approximately 5 seconds per sheet, totaling 1,500 seconds (25 minutes/day) for a 300-sheet daily volume—representing a conservative upper-bound assumption.
  * Reference Calculation: $(Daily\ Bypass\ Time\ 25\ min \times Operational\ Days\ D) \le (Accident\ Count\ N \times Downtime\ Per\ Accident\ T_{\text{downtime}})$
  * Over 250 annual operating days, cumulative bypass overhead reaches ~104 hours. A direct 1:1 mathematical comparison against collision downtime (1 to 4 hours per incident) does not fully capture financial trade-offs. The primary engineering value lies in preventing major tooling destruction, turret disk damage, cable diagnostic confusion, and operator injury risks while preserving operational continuity.

## 6. Specialized Sensor Array, Smart Terminal Connectivity, and Local AI Reserved Space
* Specialized Sensor Channel Expansion — Allocates dedicated edge channels for high-speed table motion, punching vibration, zero-point displacement, and cable continuity diagnostics.
* Zero-Point Displacement and Cable Integrity Module — Evaluates physical datum shifts caused by clamp jaw warping and detects cable strand micro-fracture signatures prior to stroke execution.
* Acoustic and Vibration Chipping Sensors — High-frequency sensors monitor punching shockwave profiles, X/Y table vibration spectra, and friction acoustics to detect dislodged metal chips.
* Smart Terminal Mesh Infrastructure — Integrates wearable security watches, smartphones, industrial PDAs, and machine smart tags via mesh protocols to cross-validate operator positioning and setup parameters.
* Local Vision AI Agent Space — Reserves computational headroom for lightweight edge vision agents analyzing sheet positioning, clamp jaw alignment, turret disk clearances, and operator hand intrusion frame-by-frame.

## 7. Ultra-Low Latency Control, Dual-Track Data Retention, and T-Reg Governance
* eFPGA Hardware Interlock Control — Implements software-independent eFPGA hardware logic targeting sub-1ms response times to trigger motor STO/SS1 shutoffs across both manual and automated modes upon detecting sensor mismatches, datum shifts, or internal cable faults.
* T-Reg Progressive Deceleration Mode — Dynamically reduces X/Y table feed rates (e.g., by 15% or variable $n\%$) when sensor noise, minor zero-point drift, impedance anomalies, or operator fatigue signatures are detected.
* International Standard Dual-Track Data Retention Policy — Complies with ISO 45001 and KOSHA-MS safety logging guidelines through pipeline separation:
  * Track 1 (Real-Time Operational Control) — Raw vision and acoustic streams are anonymized at the edge and permanently purged within 10 seconds.
  * Track 2 (Safety Telemetry & Failure Data Code Logging) — Formats collision events, sensor discrepancies, zero-point shifts, cable breakages, and near-miss logs into standardized encrypted Failure Data Codes (CBOR/Ledger) for long-term retention, supporting rule-based algorithm optimization and predictive maintenance.

## 9. Generic Concept Scope Definition
* Scope and Safety Distance Independence — The 150mm and 200mm safety line values illustrate technical principles; any bypass distance exceeding maximum die dimensions to prevent clamp interference falls within the scope disclosed in this whitepaper.
* Material and Thickness Independence — "Sheet metal" encompasses stainless steel (SUS), cold-rolled steel (CR), aluminum (AL), galvanized steel (GI), copper, brass, and other thin metallic sheets regardless of thickness.
* Controller Mode and Staffing Independence — Applies across manual (JOG/MDI/setup) and automatic (AUTO/loader-linked) modes, as well as single-operator or dedicated maintenance staffing models.
* NC Syntax (G/M Code) Independence — Specific G-code designations (such as G70) represent field examples; all G/M codes, macros, subroutines, and command structures executing non-punching moves to safety lines are disclosed as equivalent structures within the scope of this whitepaper as technical directional guidance.
* Failure Data Code Processing Independence — All software/hardware implementations converting operational anomalies into standardized data codes for dual-track storage fall within the scope disclosed in this whitepaper.
* Protocol-Independent Standalone Operation — Executes functional safety relying strictly on onboard edge circuitry and eFPGA logic independently of external communication network availability.
* Sensor and Terminal Combination Flexibility — Sensor counts, modalities, and terminal pairings represent non-limiting examples; modular additions, subtractions, or reconfigurations remain within the scope disclosed in this whitepaper.
* Multi-Sensor/Terminal Scalability ($n \ge 1$) — Applies across configurations combining single, dual, or $n+1$ heterogeneous sensors, edge nodes, and smart terminals.
* Relative Kinematics and Scale Independence — Applies across tool-moving, workpiece-moving, and combined relative-motion kinematics across all machine scales.
